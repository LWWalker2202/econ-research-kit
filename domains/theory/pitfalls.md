# Theory and mechanism design: what referees ask

The questions below are the ones that recur in reports on theory and mechanism-design papers. They are not hostile in tone; they are asking whether the model earns its assumptions. Each question is paired with the response that actually closes it, and the last section lists the failures that generate the questions in the first place.

## What referees ask

1. "What happens to your main proposition if the key assumption is relaxed, or dropped entirely?"
2. "Which assumption delivers the mechanism, and is that assumption testable or at least observable?"
3. "Is the comparative static sign derived, or is it assumed? Please show it in the text."
4. "Why should the reader believe the calibrated parameters rather than the values implied by a simpler model?"
5. "Does the model have a unique equilibrium, or does the sign of the comparative static depend on which equilibrium is selected?"
6. "What observation would make you abandon the mechanism? If none, the model is not doing empirical work."
7. "How is this different from the standard model with one additional friction, and does the addition change any prediction?"
8. "Your data moment for parameter X is measured on a different population than the model's agents. What is the mapping?"
9. "Can the welfare conclusion be implemented by a policy the government can actually commit to?"
10. "What happens in the limiting case where your friction vanishes? If the answer is the textbook model, say so and calibrate the distance."

## How to respond

- For 1 and 2: put a short robustness section in the model, not in the appendix, showing which propositions survive and which need the assumption. Name the assumption that carries the mechanism in the sentence that states the proposition.
- For 3: derive the sign under stated sufficient conditions, and report the parameter region where it holds. If the sign is ambiguous, say so and characterise the boundary rather than picking one side.
- For 4: present the moments, the admissible ranges, and the sensitivity of the main result to movement within those ranges. A calibration defended by its source and its robustness survives this question; one defended by its plausibility does not.
- For 5: prove uniqueness under stated conditions, or write down the selection rule and defend it with an argument about the institution, not about convenience.
- For 6: state the testable implication, the data that would speak to it, and what the result would have to be to reject the mechanism. Then, if the data exist, do that test in the paper.
- For 7 and 10: locate the new prediction relative to the nested model and report the limiting case. A friction that changes no prediction is decoration, and referees read it that way.
- For 8: write the mapping from data units to model units explicitly, including any aggregation assumption, and report the sensitivity to the mapping.
- For 9: check whether the policy instrument in the model exists in the institutional setting, and whether commitment is available. If it is not, discuss the second-best version rather than the first-best welfare number.

## Where it goes wrong

| Failure | How it shows up in the paper | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption doing no work | The main proposition is proved without the assumption, or the assumption appears only in a footnote | Show what breaks without it, or delete it |
| Mechanism and data disconnected | The story is verbal, the model delivers a sign, and no test connects them | State the observation that could reject the mechanism and run it if the data exist |
| Comparative-static sign asserted | The sign is claimed in the abstract and appears in the text without derivation | Derive it under explicit sufficient conditions and report the admissible region |
| Existence and uniqueness skipped | Comparative statics are stated for "the equilibrium" without a proof | Prove existence and uniqueness, or state and defend a selection rule |
| Calibration that produces the conclusion | The parameter values are chosen so that the model matches the headline fact, and no other moment is reported | Separate calibration moments from test moments, and report the fit on both |
| Policy conclusion not implementable | The optimal policy requires commitment or information the government does not have | Recast the result as second best and say which constraint binds |

The common thread is that referees are not asking for more algebra. They are asking whether each assumption, each sign, and each number in the paper could have come out differently, and whether the paper would have told the reader if it had.
