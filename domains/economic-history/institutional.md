# Economic history: institutional background

Chinese economic history is a field in which the treatment variable almost never arrives pre-measured. The institutions described below are old, but the reason a researcher needs to know them is current: each of them preserved variation in a document, and that variation is what a paper can use as treatment or as an instrument. This file records the institutions a referee will expect you to describe correctly, the dates that define the windows, and two paragraphs you can adapt for an institutional background section.

## Institutional background

**Fiscal service and population registration (fuyi, corvee and tax).** In the Ming, households were organized into lijia units and recorded in the yellow registers (huangce), which listed land and adult male labour and fixed the labour-service and tax obligations of each unit. The Single Whip reform (yitiaobianfa) converted most of those obligations into payments in silver and attached them to the land, which made the county, rather than the household, the unit that carried the quota. In the Qing, the Kangxi emperor froze the head-tax base so that additions to the registered population would not raise the fiscal claim, and a later reform merged the remaining poll tax into the land tax (tanding rumu). Fiscal obligation was thereby separated from the size of the population it nominally covered, and the county-level quotas set in this period survived into later centuries while the population they described moved. The result is a county-level measure of fiscal burden that is fixed before the outcomes a modern paper usually studies, which is why county quota data appear so often as treatment or as an instrument.

**Maritime customs and treaty ports (haiguan yu tongshang kouan).** After the 1842 treaty ended the Canton system, a set of ports was opened to foreign trade, and further ports were opened by treaty over the following decades at staggered dates. From the middle of the nineteenth century the customs service was administered by a foreign inspectorate that published annual trade returns, so the trade of each port was recorded in a consistent format while the port itself entered the customs regime at a date fixed by treaty rather than by local conditions. Tariff-setting autonomy was lost with the treaty system and restored only in the late 1920s [verify]. For identification, the opening dates provide a staggered treatment that is dated by diplomacy and not by local economic trends, and the customs returns provide the pre-period trade series needed to test the parallel-trends assumption. This is the canonical panel design in Chinese economic history.

**Planning-era archives and rationing (jihua shiqi de dangan yu piaozheng).** After 1953 the state monopolized the purchase and marketing of grain, and from the mid-1950s urban residents received ration coupons for grain, cloth, and other goods. Entitlement ran through the work unit and was tied to registered residence, so who received how much was an administrative decision recorded in unit archives and in the statistical yearbooks of the period. The archive is therefore unusually complete on the allocation side and unusually thin on prices and on the informal market, and a paper that uses the plan-era allocation must explain what it is measuring.

**Dual-track prices in early reform (jiage shuangguizhi).** In the mid-1980s enterprises were permitted to sell output above the plan quota at market prices, which created two prices for the same commodity and turned the plan quota into a measurable exposure. As the market share grew, the gap between the two prices became an indicator of how far liberalization had proceeded in a sector or locality. The exposure is administrative and dated, which makes it attractive as a dose, but the plan quota was also revised in response to shortages, so the timing argument has to be made explicitly.

## Ready-to-cite paragraphs

County-level fiscal obligations in imperial China were fixed early and revised rarely. Households were registered in the yellow registers of the Ming and organized into lijia units that carried labour-service and tax quotas, and the Single Whip reform of the late sixteenth century converted most of those obligations into silver payments assessed on land. In the Qing, the head-tax base was frozen and the remaining poll tax was merged into the land tax, so that the registered population and the fiscal claim on it no longer moved together. Because the county quotas set in this period survived into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the fiscal burden recorded for a county was determined before the economic outcomes that later work studies, and its variation across counties reflects the administrative settlement of the imperial state rather than the contemporaneous economy [to fill].

Trade in the treaty-port era is recorded in a form that is unusually favourable to panel work. The 1842 treaty ended the Canton system and opened a first set of ports, and further ports were opened by treaty at staggered dates through the second half of the nineteenth century. From the middle of the century the customs service was run by a foreign inspectorate that published annual returns, so each port's trade was measured in a comparable format from the date it entered the customs regime. Because a port's opening date was set by diplomatic agreement, it is not a response to local trade conditions, and the customs returns allow the pre-opening trend to be observed and tested. The customs series also has known breaks, most importantly the recovery of tariff autonomy in the late 1920s and the change of recording conventions after 1949, and a paper that spans these breaks has to state how it handles them [verify].
