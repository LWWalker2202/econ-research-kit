# Development economics and fieldwork: institutional background

Development work in China is rarely about a single national program. It is about a set of locally administered interventions that change who is eligible for a transfer, which village receives a project, where a school or clinic sits, and what a household may do with its land. The unit of treatment is usually the county or the village rather than the individual, and the unit of data collection is the household survey conducted by a research team. Both facts shape what can be identified and what a referee will ask.

## Institutional background

**Poverty alleviation and its targeting apparatus.** The urban minimum living standard guarantee (dibao) emerged in the late 1990s as a locally financed income floor, and the rural version was rolled out nationally in the 2000s. The rural poverty alleviation campaign that ran through the 2010s added an administrative registration of poor households, village-level work teams, and named cadres assigned to individual households. The important institutional detail for research is that eligibility is assessed locally against a discretionary standard, so the same nominal income can be treated as poor in one county and not poor in the next. A region-level or household-level eligibility threshold measures a local administrative decision, not a national rule.

**County and village boundary changes.** Converting a county into a district reallocates fiscal authority, land use rights, and public service responsibilities to the city; merging villages and closing small schools changes where services are delivered and how far households must travel. These are policy events with sharp dates and uneven geography, which is why they appear in identification designs. They are also events that change the reporting unit itself, so the researcher must confirm that the panel survives the change.

**Land rights.** Rural land is collectively owned. The 2010s land confirmation program mapped and registered household contract rights, while the separation of ownership, contract rights, and management rights formalized renting-out. For research, the relevant variation is timing across provinces and counties, and the outcome is usually rental participation, investment, or migration rather than a simple productivity measure.

**Public services.** The rural cooperative medical scheme was piloted in the mid-2000s and expanded nationally, and the reform that exempted rural students from tuition and textbook fees followed a similar staggered rollout. Both are usable as access shocks, and both were implemented by local governments with different intensity. School consolidation in the 2000s was justified by falling enrollment in villages; it is a treatment for schooling outcomes and a confounder for anything measured at the village level.

**Credit and digital finance.** Microcredit pilots gave selected counties access to subsidized group lending, and the digital inclusive finance index built from payment platform data provides a county-year measure of financial access. The first is a program with a selection rule; the second is a continuous exposure measure with its own construction choices.

**Relocation.** The relocation of households out of ecologically fragile or extremely poor areas during the poverty campaign moved entire villages and created new resettlement communities. It produces both a treatment and a sample problem: relocated households are often lost from village rosters, and the destination community is not comparable to the origin community.

**Cadre incentives.** Local officials are evaluated on targets that include poverty reduction, and village work teams are judged on measured outcomes. This makes reporting an equilibrium outcome, not an independent measurement. Any claim that a program reduced measured poverty should address whether the measurement was produced by the same administration that was evaluated on it.

## Ready-to-cite paragraphs

Poverty governance in rural China combines a nationally set goal with locally administered eligibility. Households are registered as poor by village-level procedures against a standard that is assessed locally, and registered households are then attached to named cadres and to village-level work teams that are evaluated on whether measured outcomes improve. Because eligibility is a local administrative decision rather than a rule applied uniformly across counties, two households with the same income in different counties need not receive the same treatment, and the county is the natural unit at which the policy is observed. The campaign that began in the mid-2010s intensified this system and ended with the closing of the registration in 2020 [verify], which defines the window over which retrospective evaluations can use it.

Land and public services provide complementary margins. Rural land is collectively owned, and the confirmation program of the 2010s registered household contract rights and enabled renting-out, so the timing of registration across counties is the source of variation for land-related outcomes. Health insurance and schooling were expanded through staggered local rollouts rather than a single national date, and school consolidation in the 2000s changed the distance between households and schools. In all three cases the effective treatment varies by county and by village, and this paper measures it at that level [to fill]. Local cadre evaluation is a further institutional feature: village work teams and county officials are assessed on measured poverty reduction, so reported outcomes are produced under administrative incentives and should be interpreted with that in mind.
