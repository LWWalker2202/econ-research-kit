# Labor economics: institutional background

China's labor market is not one market. It is a set of segmented markets whose boundaries are drawn by registration status, locality, sector, and firm formality, and most credible labor papers set in China identify their effects off a policy change that moves one of those boundaries. This file records the institutions a referee will expect you to describe correctly, the dates that define reform windows, and two paragraphs you can adapt for an Institutional Background section.

## Institutional background

**Household registration (hukou) and migration.** The hukou system assigns every person a registered locality and an agricultural or non-agricultural status. Public services such as schooling, health insurance, and parts of social assistance are administered where a person is registered rather than where they work, so a rural resident who moves to a city usually works without local registration and faces a different effective wage and benefit package even at identical productivity. Reform since the 2010s has moved settlement rules toward city-specific thresholds: smaller cities relaxed earlier and more fully, while large cities kept points-based or quota systems. The result is cross-province and cross-city-tier variation in the cost of migration and in who is eligible to convert status, which is the raw material for most migration and hukou designs.

**Minimum wage.** Minimum wages are set below the national level, mainly by provincial governments and in some cases by municipalities, and are revised at irregular intervals rather than on a fixed calendar. The level, the timing of each revision, and the bite relative to the local average wage all differ by province. The statutory floor is therefore usable as a treatment variable, but only after the paper shows that covered workers exist in the sample and that the floor is likely to bind for them.

**Social insurance contributions.** Employers and employees are required to contribute to pension, health, unemployment, work-injury, and maternity insurance. Nominal rates sit in national bands, but the contribution base, collection intensity, and the share of firms that actually enroll workers vary substantially across localities and between formal and informal employers. Employer contribution-rate reductions and the transfer of collection to the tax administration are the two changes most often used as labor-cost shocks.

**Labor Contract Law.** The 2008 Labor Contract Law required written contracts, set severance obligations, and tightened the rules on temporary and dispatched labor. Compliance varied with local enforcement and firm characteristics. Credible designs therefore interact the law with an ex ante measure of exposure, such as the pre-reform share of temporary workers or firm turnover, rather than relying on a simple before-after comparison.

**State-owned enterprise reform and xiagang.** The restructuring of state-owned enterprises in the late 1990s separated a large number of urban workers from their jobs and produced the administrative category of xiagang (laid-off) workers, who kept a formal tie to the enterprise without working. Exposure was concentrated by industry, region, and birth cohort, and the shock remains a workhorse source of variation for labor supply, health, and savings outcomes.

**Retirement.** Statutory retirement ages are low by international standards and differ by gender and by occupation, and pension schemes are segmented between urban employees, urban residents, and rural residents. A decision to raise retirement ages gradually was announced in 2024 [verify]. That reform creates a forward-looking policy window; it is not yet a source of retrospective evaluation.

## Ready-to-cite paragraphs

China's labor market is segmented by the household registration (hukou) system. A person's hukou is tied to a locality and to an agricultural or non-agricultural status, and access to publicly provided schooling, health insurance, and some social assistance is administered where that person is registered rather than where they work. Rural residents who move to cities therefore typically work without local registration, and the gap between registered and non-registered workers in the same city has been a persistent feature of the labor market. Since the 2010s, settlement rules have been progressively relaxed, but relaxation has been differentiated by city size and tier: smaller cities removed restrictions earlier and more fully, while large cities retained points-based or quota systems. The cost of migration and the eligibility for local registration thus vary across provinces and city tiers and have changed at different times, which is the institutional basis for the exposure measures used in this paper [to fill].

On the employment-protection side, three institutions define the relevant margins. Minimum wages are set by provincial governments, and in some municipalities by municipal governments, and are revised at irregular intervals, so the statutory floor differs across provinces and over time. The 2008 Labor Contract Law required written contracts, set severance obligations, and tightened the rules on temporary and dispatched labor, while compliance varied with local enforcement and employer characteristics. Social insurance contributions are nominally required for pension, health, unemployment, work-injury, and maternity insurance, but the contribution base and the share of enrolled workers differ substantially between formal and informal employers. Finally, statutory retirement ages are low by international standards, differ by gender and occupation, and are scheduled to rise gradually following the 2024 decision [verify]. These institutions make wage floors, job protection, labor cost, and retirement timing separate sources of variation rather than one labor-market regime.
