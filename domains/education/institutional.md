# Education economics: institutional background

Education in China is a sequence of cohort-specific rules. A child's schooling is governed by a law that expanded in scope over time, financed through a fiscal system that shifted responsibility from villages to counties to the center, and allocated across tracks by examinations whose difficulty and accessibility depend on where the child lives. Because each of these arrangements changed at a known date and applied to identifiable cohorts or counties, the field has an unusually rich set of natural experiments. This file records the institutions, the dates, two paragraphs that can be lifted into a paper, and the sources a referee will expect.

## Institutional background

**Compulsory education and fee exemptions.** The Compulsory Education Law of 1986 established nine years of schooling as a legal obligation, but implementation was gradual and many rural families continued to pay tuition, textbook, and miscellaneous fees. The "two exemptions and one subsidy" policy, introduced in the early 2000s and phased in by region and by grade, removed tuition and textbook fees for rural students and subsidized boarding. The phase-in schedule, together with the 2006 revision of the law and the later extension of fee exemptions to urban schools, gives cohort-by-region variation that is the backbone of most work on the returns to schooling in China.

**Rural school consolidation.** Starting in 2001, local governments merged or closed small village primary schools and moved students to larger township or county schools, often with boarding. The reform was implemented by county governments, so its timing and intensity vary across counties and can be measured from county-level school counts. It is used to study attainment, the costs of longer commuting, and the effect of school resources on learning.

**College expansion.** In 1999 the central government sharply increased higher-education enrollment quotas, and the expansion continued through the following decade. Because the increase applied to cohorts graduating from high school in particular years, exposure can be defined as the interaction between a birth cohort and the province-specific intensity of the expansion. The setting is used for returns to education, inequality, and the quality-quantity trade-off.

**Vocational and academic tracking.** At the end of lower secondary school, students are allocated between academic high schools and vocational schools, with admission determined by examination scores and by quotas that differ across provinces. The tracking rule creates a discontinuity in school type at a score threshold, and it also generates the selection problem that any study of vocational returns must confront.

**Migrant children and examination access.** Access to the national college entrance examination is tied to household registration, so children who migrate with their parents face restrictions where they live. Provinces began to announce local schemes allowing eligible migrant children to sit the examination where they reside, with eligibility conditions that differ across provinces and years. The rollout gives province-by-year variation in access, but the eligibility rules are themselves a function of local political decisions.

**Fiscal decentralization and per-student funding.** Responsibility for financing compulsory education shifted over the 1980s and 1990s toward local governments, and in the 2000s toward a county-based system with central subsidies. Per-student recurrent expenditure therefore varies across counties and over time, and the variation is used to estimate the effect of school resources on enrollment and achievement.

**After-school tutoring and the 2021 restrictions.** A 2021 policy sharply restricted for-profit after-school academic tutoring and reduced homework. Because the restrictions were implemented through local education bureaus with different intensity, they provide recent variation for work on shadow education, educational inequality, and household time allocation.

## Ready-to-cite paragraphs

China's Compulsory Education Law of 1986 made nine years of schooling a legal obligation, but the law did not by itself remove the private costs of attendance. Rural families continued to pay tuition, textbook, and miscellaneous fees throughout the 1990s, and enrollment and completion differed sharply between richer and poorer counties. Beginning in the early 2000s, the central government introduced the "two exemptions and one subsidy" program, which waived tuition and textbook fees and provided living subsidies to boarding students, and phased it in by region and grade before extending fee exemptions to all rural and later urban schools. The result is a sequence of cohort- and region-specific reductions in the cost of schooling that can be dated from central and provincial documents and matched to household survey respondents by birth year and place of residence.

The supply side of Chinese schooling changed at the same time. County governments, which became the main financiers of compulsory education in the 2000s, consolidated small village schools into larger township and county schools, often with boarding facilities, in order to raise the quality of instruction and reduce the number of under-resourced rural schools. Because consolidation was decided and implemented at the county level, the timing and the intensity of school closures vary across counties in ways that are observable in county education statistics. This variation is the basis of a large literature on how school accessibility and school resources affect enrollment, completed schooling, and later labor market outcomes.
