# Public economics: institutional background

China's fiscal system is a useful setting for policy evaluation because the central government sets rules that bind every locality while the implementation and the money flow through layers of government that differ in capacity. The variation a researcher exploits is rarely a single national reform; it is the interaction between a national rule and the local conditions under which the rule binds. This file records the institutional features that generate that variation, the timeline along which they changed, two paragraphs that can be lifted into an Institutional Background section, and the sources a referee will expect to see cited.

## Institutional background

**Fiscal contracting and tax sharing.** The 1994 reform replaced the earlier revenue-sharing arrangements with a system that assigns taxes to the central and local governments and divides shared taxes by fixed rules. Local governments kept responsibility for a large share of expenditure while their revenue base was narrowed, which created both a fiscal gap and a strong incentive to expand the tax base. Researchers use the reform for two purposes: as a structural break in local incentives, and as the origin of the vertical fiscal imbalance that later transfer policy tries to fill. Sharing ratios were adjusted over time and differ across taxes, which is where the identifying variation usually comes from.

**Transfer payments.** Transfers come as general transfers allocated by formula and as earmarked transfers tied to specific programs. Formula-based allocation is the more attractive source of variation because the formula is written down and can be reconstructed, whereas earmarked allocation is a bargaining outcome that is correlated with local need. Changes in the formula, in the size of the pool, and in the matching requirements attached to a program are the events researchers typically date. The key distinction in the literature is between the income effect of receiving more money and the incentive effect of the rules attached to it.

**The VAT reform.** The replacement of the business tax by value-added tax, piloted in selected sectors and later extended nationwide, changed both the tax burden of firms and the revenue available to local governments. It is attractive for firm-level work because the pilot rolled out by sector and region in a staggered way, and unattractive because the timing of a locality's inclusion is not random.

**Social assistance and social insurance.** Minimum living guarantee standards are set locally within national guidelines, and central transfers subsidize part of the cost. This produces cross-county variation in benefit levels and in coverage that is often used to study consumption, labor supply, and targeting. Social insurance contribution rates and enforcement also differ across localities, which matters whenever the outcome is a labor cost.

**Fiscal transparency and information disclosure.** Budget disclosure practices differ across provinces and municipalities and changed at different dates. Where disclosure is the treatment, the researcher has to argue that the date of disclosure is not driven by the outcome being studied.

**Local government debt.** Local governments borrow through financing vehicles whose obligations are not on the published budget, and the rules governing that borrowing changed repeatedly. Debt studies therefore depend on reconstructing off-budget flows, and the measurement problem is usually more serious than the identification problem.

## Ready-to-cite paragraphs

China's fiscal system concentrates revenue authority at the central level while leaving a large share of expenditure responsibility with local governments. The 1994 tax-sharing reform fixed the division of major taxes and, by narrowing the local revenue base without reducing local expenditure obligations, created the vertical fiscal gap that subsequent transfer policy has attempted to close. Because the same national rules apply to jurisdictions with very different tax bases and expenditure needs, the intensity with which the rules bind varies across places, and this variation is the raw material for most identification in the field.

Transfers from the central government reach localities through two channels. General transfers are allocated by formula and are, in principle, predictable from published rules and local characteristics, while earmarked transfers are tied to specific programs and allocated through a process that is only partly formulaic. This distinction matters for empirical work: a change in the general transfer formula provides a source of variation that can be reconstructed from public documents, whereas a change in earmarked allocations is difficult to separate from the local political and economic conditions that also determine the outcome. Studies that exploit transfer variation therefore usually begin by documenting which channel changed, when, and according to what rule.
