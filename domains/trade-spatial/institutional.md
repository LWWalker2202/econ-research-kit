# International Trade and Spatial Economics: Institutional Background

## Institutional background

Chinese trade and spatial policy has two layers, and empirical work usually keeps them apart. The first is the trade regime: tariffs, non-tariff barriers, export tax rebates, the exchange rate, and the processing-trade arrangements that decide which imports enter duty-free. The second is the spatial regime: zones, transport networks, and administrative boundaries that decide where production locates and how far goods and people move in a day. A paper needs the layer where the treatment arrives and must control for the other one.

The trade regime long predates WTO accession. 经济特区 (special economic zones) were created in 1980 in Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen; fourteen coastal cities were opened in 1984; 上海浦东新区 (Pudong New Area) followed in 1990. Tariffs were cut unilaterally through the 1990s and the exchange rate was unified in 1994. Accession to the WTO in December 2001 made these cuts binding: the bound tariff schedule was cut in steps, quantitative restrictions and licensing on most manufactured goods were eliminated, and trading rights were extended to private firms. Because the liberalization was negotiated product by product and applied nationally, the usable variation after 2001 is product-level exposure, not a province dummy.

加工贸易 (processing trade) is the second feature of the regime and easy to miss. Inputs imported for processing exports enter under bonded arrangements and are exempt from duty, first through 保税区 (bonded zones, from 外高桥 in Shanghai in 1990), then through 出口加工区 (export processing zones, from 2000), and later through 综合保税区 (integrated bonded zones). Ordinary trade pays the tariff, so a tariff cut bites ordinary imports directly and reaches processing imports only through the cost of domestic content and the choice between regimes. Customs statistics record the trade mode, so the two flows can be separated at the transaction level but not in a city aggregate.

The spatial regime has its own calendar. 经济技术开发区 (economic and technological development zones) were set up from 1984, 高新技术产业开发区 (high-tech development zones) from the late 1980s, and a consolidation round in the mid-2000s closed or absorbed many county-level zones [verify]. 自由贸易试验区 (pilot free trade zones) started with 上海 in 2013 and were extended in batches to most coastal provinces and several inland hubs; 跨境电子商务综合试验区 (cross-border e-commerce pilot zones) started with 杭州 in 2015. Transport expanded at the same time: the first high-speed rail line, 京津城际, opened in 2008, and the 八纵八横 (eight vertical, eight horizontal) corridor plan was issued in 2016. The Belt and Road Initiative (一带一路), announced in 2013, added 中欧班列 (China-Europe Railway Express) services and overseas port and rail projects.

Boundaries move as well. 撤县设区 (county-to-district conversions) attach a county to a larger city and change both numerator and denominator of any city-level rate; county-level cities are upgraded or merged; yearbooks report 全市 (the whole prefecture) and 市辖区 (urban districts) as different populations; and 开发区 are upgraded from provincial to national status, changing tax treatment without moving a firm.

For identification, the unit where exposure is defined (product, industry, county, city, firm), the unit where the policy binds (national tariff schedule, provincial designation, city transport node), and the unit where the outcome is measured are three different things. Overlapping instruments are the rule, since an FTZ city also got a rail station and also sits in a coastal province exposed to import competition.

## Timeline

| Year | Event | Why it matters for research |
|---|---|---|
| 1980 | Special economic zones in Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen | First place-based openness treatment, with a clean start date |
| 1984 | Fourteen coastal cities opened; first economic and technological development zones | City-level designation that can be matched to later trade and firm data |
| 1990 | Pudong New Area and the first bonded zone | Start of the bonded regime that makes processing imports duty-exempt |
| 1994 | Exchange rate unified; tax reform | Nominal and fiscal shocks that overlap with trade exposure |
| 2001 | WTO accession in December | Product-level tariff cuts, removal of quotas |
| 2008 | First high-speed rail line (Beijing-Tianjin) opens | Start of the transport-accessibility literature in China |
| 2013 | Shanghai pilot free trade zone; Belt and Road Initiative announced | Staggered designation plus outward-facing policy |
| 2015 | First cross-border e-commerce pilot zone (Hangzhou) | City-level designation arriving after the WTO tariff cuts |
| 2016 | Eight-vertical eight-horizontal rail corridor plan | Fixes the network used by travel-time measures |
| 2020 | Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership signed | New tariff regime, mostly outside outcome windows |

## Ready-to-cite paragraphs

The paragraphs below can be pasted into an Institutional Background section; check the dates against the documents under Sources.

China's integration into world markets was built in two layers. The trade regime was liberalized first unilaterally, through tariff cuts, the unification of the exchange rate in 1994, and the special regimes granted to special economic zones from 1980 and to coastal cities from 1984, and then multilaterally: accession to the World Trade Organization in December 2001 made the tariff schedule binding, removed quantitative restrictions on most manufactured goods, and extended trading rights to private firms. Much of manufactured exports moves under processing arrangements, in which imported inputs enter duty-free and are re-exported after assembly, so tariffs bind ordinary imports directly and processing imports only indirectly. Because the commitments were negotiated product by product and applied uniformly across the country, the variation created by accession is product-level exposure rather than a province-level policy.

The spatial layer operated on a separate calendar and through different instruments. Place-based designation moved from special economic zones in 1980 and coastal cities in 1984 to economic and technological development zones, high-tech development zones, pilot free trade zones from Shanghai in 2013, and cross-border e-commerce pilot zones from 2015. Transport accessibility changed as the high-speed rail network expanded from the Beijing-Tianjin line in 2008 and as the eight-vertical eight-horizontal corridor plan of 2016 fixed the long-run network. Administrative boundaries moved too: counties were converted into districts of larger cities, and county-level cities were upgraded or merged. Any measure of market access, agglomeration, or the border effect is therefore built on spatial units and travel times that are themselves changing during the sample period.
